    More ways to lower college costs

    Scholarships are one piece of paying for college. Students and families can often reduce the total bill by comparing the real cost of schools, asking about institutional aid, planning housing and course choices, and understanding which forms of aid must eventually be repaid.

    Start with the net price—not the advertised tuition. A college's published cost is often called its sticker price. Your net price is the amount left after grants and scholarships that you do not have to repay. Compare the full cost of attendance, including tuition, fees, housing, food, books, transportation, and personal expenses.

    U.S. Department of Education: Net Price Calculator Center ↗ · How to compare financial aid offers ↗

    1. Complete the FAFSA every year

    The Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) is the starting point for many federal, state, and college financial aid programs. It is free to submit. Students generally need to complete a new FAFSA for each academic year they want aid.

    • Use the official StudentAid.gov website—not a paid imitation.
    • Check federal, state, and college deadlines. State and school deadlines can be earlier than the federal deadline.
    • Review your FAFSA Submission Summary and correct errors if needed.
    • Make sure every college you are considering receives your FAFSA information.

    FAFSA information helps determine eligibility; it does not guarantee a particular grant or scholarship.

    2. Ask colleges about more institutional aid

    Colleges may have their own merit scholarships, need-based grants, department awards, honors-program awards, emergency grants, and transfer scholarships. Some opportunities require a separate application.

    • Ask the financial aid office what additional grants or scholarships are available.
    • Ask your academic department about awards for your major or year in school.
    • If your family's financial situation changed—such as job loss, a major medical expense, or a change in income—ask whether the school can review your aid due to special circumstances.
    • Ask whether an outside scholarship changes your existing aid package and how the school applies it.

    A polite appeal is not a guarantee of additional aid, but it can be worth asking.

    3. Compare colleges before committing

    A school with a higher sticker price can sometimes cost less after grants and scholarships. Compare the same items for every school and ask whether awards renew in later years.

    • Use each college's net price calculator as an estimate—not a final financial aid offer.
    • Compare tuition, mandatory fees, housing, meals, books, travel, and other living costs.
    • Check whether scholarships require a minimum GPA, full-time enrollment, or a specific major.
    • Ask how many years an award lasts and what is required to renew it.

    Compare schools with College Scorecard ↗

    4. Explore in-state tuition and residency rules

    Public universities often charge different rates for in-state and out-of-state students. Some states and regions have tuition reciprocity or exchange programs, but eligibility rules vary.

    • Ask the college's residency office which rules apply before assuming you qualify.
    • Check regional exchange programs such as the Western Undergraduate Exchange if you are considering participating western states.
    • Ask whether your state has grants that can be used at eligible out-of-state schools.
    • Do not assume moving to a state for college automatically makes you an in-state resident.

    5. Consider community college and transfer pathways

    Starting at a community college and transferring can reduce costs for some students, especially when they can live at home. The key is making sure credits apply to the intended bachelor's degree.

    • Ask for a written transfer or articulation agreement when available.
    • Before taking a course, confirm with the receiving college how it will transfer and count toward your major.
    • Compare the total cost of both stages, including fees, transportation, and housing.
    • Ask about transfer scholarships and application deadlines at the destination school.
    Important: A course transferring as elective credit does not necessarily mean it satisfies a major requirement. Get the answer from the receiving college in writing.

    6. Reduce everyday college expenses

    Small decisions can add up over multiple semesters. Compare the savings with the time, transportation, and academic impact before choosing an option.

    • Compare living at home, commuting, residence halls, and off-campus housing.
    • Ask whether you can rent, buy used, borrow, or use free/open educational resources instead of buying every textbook new.
    • Review meal plans and avoid paying for more meals than you realistically use.
    • Use student discounts, public transit, campus resources, and free software when available.
    • Plan course schedules carefully to avoid unnecessary extra semesters—but do not overload yourself to the point that it harms your progress.

    7. Look at work, employer benefits, and paid experience

    Part-time work, paid internships, co-ops, and employer education benefits may help cover expenses. Federal Work-Study is a job program, not a grant paid automatically to your tuition account.

    • Ask the financial aid office how work-study jobs are found and how wages are paid.
    • Check whether your employer offers tuition assistance or reimbursement and what conditions apply.
    • Look for paid internships related to your field.
    • Build a realistic school-year work schedule that leaves time for classes and studying.

    Do not count on a work-study award as money you already have: you generally must work the hours to earn the wages.

    8. Ask about payment plans and billing options

    Some colleges let students spread a semester bill across installments. A payment plan may help with budgeting, but check enrollment fees, payment dates, missed-payment penalties, and whether the plan charges interest.

    • Contact the bursar or student accounts office before a bill is due.
    • Ask about installment plans, emergency aid, short-term institutional loans, and payment deadlines.
    • Understand what happens if a balance remains unpaid, including late fees or registration holds.
    • Avoid putting large tuition balances on high-interest credit cards without understanding the cost.

    Understanding college debt and student loans

    Loans can make it possible to attend college, but borrowed money is not a scholarship. A loan is money that must be repaid, usually with interest. Students and families should understand who is legally responsible, how interest works, and what repayment could look like before accepting a loan.

    What is the difference between aid types?

    • Scholarships and grants: Usually do not need to be repaid if you follow the award rules.
    • Work-study: Money earned by working in an eligible job; it is not automatically credited as free money.
    • Federal student loans: Borrowed funds that must be repaid with interest under the loan terms.
    • Private student loans: Loans from banks, credit unions, or other private lenders. Terms, rates, protections, and cosigner requirements vary.
    • Parent loans: A parent who borrows is responsible for repaying that loan; it does not automatically become the student's debt.

    Federal Student Aid: Types of aid ↗

    Subsidized vs. unsubsidized federal loans

    Direct Subsidized Loans are for eligible undergraduate students with financial need. The federal government generally pays interest during qualifying in-school periods, the grace period, and deferment.

    Direct Unsubsidized Loans are not based on financial need. Interest starts accruing after the loan is disbursed, including while the student is in school. If unpaid, interest may capitalize in certain circumstances, increasing the balance on which future interest is calculated.

    Compare federal loan types ↗

    Understand interest and the total amount borrowed

    Interest is the cost of borrowing money. Federal student loan rates are set by loan type and disbursement period; private loan rates and terms depend on the lender and borrower. Fixed rates generally stay the same for that loan, while variable rates can change.

    • Check the interest rate, origination fees, and amount actually disbursed.
    • Remember that borrowing each semester or year adds to the total balance.
    • Unpaid interest can increase what you owe, depending on the loan and circumstances.
    • Paying accrued interest while in school, if affordable, may reduce the amount that grows.

    Borrow carefully and compare lenders

    • Start with grants and scholarships, then consider savings and earnings, before deciding how much to borrow.
    • Review available federal student loan options before private loans; federal loans generally have standardized protections and repayment options that private loans may not offer.
    • Borrow only what is needed for eligible education costs—not the maximum simply because it is offered.
    • For private loans, compare APR, fixed vs. variable rates, fees, cosigner release, hardship options, and total repayment cost.
    • Make sure the student and any cosigner understand who is responsible if payments are missed.
    Family reminder: A parent who signs for a Parent PLUS or private parent loan is the borrower. A student who has a private loan cosigner may also create a legal repayment obligation for that cosigner under the loan contract.

    A first-time college family checklist

    If you are a parent or student navigating college costs for the first time, use this checklist to organize the process.

    Before choosing a college

    • Complete the FAFSA and check state and school deadlines.
    • Run each school's net price calculator.
    • Compare grants and scholarships separately from loans and work-study.
    • Ask whether scholarships renew and what GPA or enrollment rules apply.
    • Estimate transportation, housing, meals, books, and personal expenses.

    Before signing or borrowing

    • Read the full financial aid offer and identify every loan.
    • Calculate the remaining bill after grants and scholarships.
    • Decide how much can be paid from savings or current income.
    • Estimate the total debt after all planned years—not just the first semester.
    • Discuss openly who is borrowing and who is responsible for repayment.

    Every semester

    • Check the student account and financial aid portal.
    • Look for new departmental, local, and continuing-student scholarships.
    • Keep copies of award letters, loan disclosures, and payment receipts.
    • Review spending and update the school-year budget.
    • Contact the aid office early if the bill cannot be paid.

    Know these important terms

    Cost of attendance
    School's estimated full yearly cost.
    Net price
    Cost minus grants and scholarships.
    Principal
    Original amount borrowed, before interest.
    APR
    Annualized borrowing cost including certain fees.
    Cosigner
    Person who agrees to be responsible under a loan contract.
    Capitalization
    Unpaid interest added to the loan principal.
